How to Write ChatGPT Prompts That Actually Work

Published August 2026 · By PromptVault Team

Most people's first prompt looks something like "write me a blog post about coffee" — and then they're disappointed when the result reads like a Wikipedia summary. The problem isn't the model. It's that vague instructions produce vague output, every single time, without exception.

The good news is that reliable prompts follow a pattern. Once you internalize it, you stop guessing and start getting output you can actually use. This guide breaks down the four parts that make a prompt work, then shows five before-and-after examples you can adapt today.

The Four Parts of a Good Prompt

Every prompt that consistently produces good results contains some combination of four elements. You don't always need all four — a quick question might only need context — but the more demanding the task, the more of these you want to include.

1. Role

Tell the model who to be. This sounds trivial, but it shapes the vocabulary, assumptions, and depth of the entire response. "You are a senior copywriter" produces different work than "you are a helpful assistant."

The role should be specific and relevant to the task. "You are a tax accountant familiar with U.S. small-business deductions" is better than "you are an accountant." The role sets the ceiling on the expertise the model brings to bear.

2. Context

Context is everything the model needs to know that isn't obvious from the task itself. Who is the audience? What's the background? What are you trying to accomplish? What constraints exist in the real world?

Context is where most prompts fail. People assume the model knows their situation. It doesn't. Every conversation starts from zero. If you're writing an email to a frustrated customer, say so — and say what the customer is frustrated about.

3. Format

Specify the shape of the output: a bulleted list, a table, an email with a subject line, three paragraphs under 100 words each, a numbered step-by-step. Left unspecified, the model guesses, and its guess is usually a generic essay.

Format instructions are the cheapest improvement you can make. They cost you nothing to write and dramatically reduce the editing you do afterward.

4. Constraints

Constraints tell the model what to avoid or limit: tone (formal, casual, confident but not salesy), length (no more than 150 words), what to include (a specific keyword, a call to action) and what to exclude (no jargon, no exclamation points, no bullet points).

Think of constraints as guardrails. They prevent the model from drifting into the default patterns that make AI writing feel generic — hedging, over-explaining, and listing everything in threes.

Five Before-and-After Examples

Here are five real prompts, rewritten using all four parts. Notice how the "after" version leaves almost nothing to guesswork.

Example 1: Replying to a customer email

Before: "Write a reply to this angry customer."

After: "You are a customer support lead at a small software company. A customer is angry because their order shipped to the wrong address and they've waited a week. Write a reply that apologizes without making excuses, explains that we've already reshipped to the correct address with expedited shipping, and offers a 20% credit on their next order. Keep it under 120 words, professional but warm, no corporate jargon. Include the subject line."

Example 2: Summarizing a long document

Before: "Summarize this article."

After: "You are a research assistant. Summarize the attached article for a busy executive who has two minutes to read it. Give me a one-paragraph summary under 80 words, then three bullet points covering the key decisions or recommendations, then a single line on anything still uncertain. Skip background and definitions — assume the reader knows the industry."

Example 3: Brainstorming content ideas

Before: "Give me blog post ideas about running."

After: "You are a content strategist for a running blog aimed at beginners in their 30s and 40s who want to run their first 5K. Give me 15 blog post ideas, sorted into three groups: motivation, training plans, and gear. For each idea, include a one-line angle that makes it different from the obvious version. Avoid generic topics like 'benefits of running.'"

Example 4: Explaining a technical concept

Before: "Explain APIs."

After: "You are a patient teacher. Explain what an API is to a small-business owner who has never written code but uses tools like Shopify and Mailchimp. Use a concrete analogy involving a restaurant, keep it under 200 words, and end with one sentence on why understanding APIs could save them money on software."

Example 5: Writing a product description

Before: "Write a product description for a water bottle."

After: "You are an e-commerce copywriter. Write a product description for a stainless steel insulated water bottle (24 oz, keeps drinks cold 24 hours) sold to hikers and gym-goers. Format: one punchy headline, a short paragraph under 60 words focused on the benefit not the feature, then four short bullet points. Tone: energetic but not hypey. Include the word 'leak-proof' once. No emoji."

A Quick Way to Remember It

Before you hit send, ask yourself four questions: Who should it act as? What does it need to know? What shape should the answer take? What should it avoid? If you can answer all four, your prompt is already in the top tier of what most people write — and your results will reflect that.

The difference between a frustrating prompt and a useful one is almost never the model. It's the fifteen seconds you spend adding context and constraints before you start. Once that becomes a habit, you stop fighting the tool and start getting real work done.
